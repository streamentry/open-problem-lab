# Primary-Care Water And Hand-Hygiene Continuity In Low- And Middle-Income Health Facilities

## Problem Statement

Water and hand hygiene in a health facility are often recorded as a yes-or-no amenity: an improved source exists, a toilet is present, or soap and water are observed during a visit. Those measures are useful service-level indicators, but they do not establish that water is available when care is delivered, that the source provides enough quantity, that stored water remains safe, or that a hand-hygiene station works at every relevant point of care.

The sharper problem is:

> Can a low- or middle-income country measure when-needed water and hand-hygiene continuity at primary-care facilities well enough to decide whether the binding constraint is source infrastructure, storage or pumping, consumables, maintenance, service-area design, or missing measurement?

This pack focuses on primary-care facilities and on the water and hand-hygiene layers that support routine care. Sanitation, environmental cleaning, health-care waste, electricity, patient experience, and health-care-associated infection outcomes are adjacent measurement families. They may be recorded as context, but they must not be silently folded into a single WASH score or treated as outcomes of water investment.

## Decision Wedge

The first decision-maker is a Ministry of Health primary-health-care, WASH, or infection-prevention director working with a district health manager and a health-system or WASH funder. The decision is whether a named group of primary-care facilities needs:

1. a new or improved water source;
2. storage, pumping, plumbing, or point-of-care distribution;
3. soap, alcohol-based hand rub, hand-drying, or replenishment support;
4. maintenance, repair, supervision, or supply-chain changes; or
5. a better facility monitoring system before capital allocation.

Evidence changes that decision only if it distinguishes:

- an improved source from water available on premises and available when needed;
- water presence from quantity, quality, storage, pump, pipe, and repair status;
- hand-hygiene station presence from function, supplies, location, and use at named points of care;
- a facility audit from a patient or population access estimate;
- one-time observation from repeated continuity, stock-out, or outage records;
- a WASH or IPC process measure from a causal clinical outcome; and
- missing, refused, not-observed, and not-applicable states.

The first contribution is therefore a measurement and source crosswalk, not a plumbing design, procurement order, infection-prevention protocol, or facility ranking.

## Strongest Objection And Response

**Objection:** WHO, UNICEF, the Joint Monitoring Programme, HHFA, and WASH FIT already provide a global monitoring system and implementation tools. A new pack could duplicate an established field or imply that no one is acting.

**Response:** The objection is valid about institutional attention. It is not sufficient for the decision at issue. The 2024 WHO/UNICEF update could not produce global estimates of basic service for its 2023 reference year because the available data were ageing, even though it could report selected no-service estimates and older 2022 basic-service estimates (evidence: `jmp-wash-hcf-2024`). The 2025 country tracker covered 107 countries, but only 17% reported sufficient financing to improve and sustain WASH and waste services (evidence: `who-unicef-wash-hcf-tracker-2025`). Existing standards and tools create a tractable measurement path; they do not prove that static facility indicators are comparable to daily availability, water quality, stock-outs, or repair time at primary-care points of care. A negative result showing that one named country already has these joins would be useful and should stop this pack from inventing another index.

## Known Facts

- **Verified fact:** The WHO/UNICEF 2023 data update reports that approximately 743 million people were served by health-care facilities with no water service in 2023; it also reports approximately 660 million people with no sanitation service and 722 million with no hygiene service. These no-service estimates are not a measure of primary-care-only exposure, continuous availability, water safety, or clinical harm (evidence: `jmp-wash-hcf-2024`).
- **Verified fact:** The same WHO/UNICEF update had enough data to estimate that 78% of health-care facilities had basic water services and 57% had basic hygiene services in 2022, but ageing data prevented global estimates of basic service for the report's 2023 reference year (evidence: `jmp-wash-hcf-2024`).
- **Verified fact:** The WHO/UNICEF core-question guideline defines candidate service ladders for water, sanitation, hand hygiene, health-care waste, and environmental cleaning, making service type, location, usability, and point-of-care observations explicit (evidence: `who-hcf-wash-core-2018`).
- **Verified fact:** The HHFA framework uses facility audits, interviews, observation, record review, sampling guidance, and an indicator inventory to assess service availability, readiness, quality, management, and finance. It is a measurement framework, not evidence that a facility operates continuously between visits (evidence: `who-hhfa-comprehensive-guide-2023`, `who-hhfa-combined-questionnaire-2023`).
- **Verified fact:** A primary cross-sectional study of 1,318 randomly selected rural facilities in Ethiopia, Kenya, Mozambique, Rwanda, Uganda, and Zambia measured access, continuity, quality, quantity, and reliability and tested drinking-water samples for _E. coli_. Fewer than 25% of facilities in each country reported that water, soap, and hand-drying materials were always available together (evidence: `guo-rural-hcf-wash-2017`).
- **Verified fact:** A 2021 analysis of health-facility surveys from 18 sub-Saharan African countries estimated 88% of facilities had an improved water source on premises, 94% an improved toilet, 74% soap plus running water or alcohol-based hand rub, and 17% standard infection-prevention precautions. The authors could not evaluate the newer WHO/UNICEF basic-service definitions because the underlying surveys lacked sufficient compatible data (evidence: `kanyangarara-wash-hcf-2021`).
- **Verified fact:** In a three-facility Togo pilot, WASH FIT assessments were repeated at baseline, midline, and endline; the average percentage of indicators meeting standards increased from 18% to 44% over the evaluated period. The pilot was not randomized or representative, and one facility's hygiene and sanitation scores declined by endline (evidence: `washfit-togo-pilot-2018`).
- **Verified fact:** WHO and UNICEF's 2025 tracker covered 107 countries, reported that over 90% had undertaken baseline assessments or developed standards, and reported sufficient financing in only 17% of countries. These are country-action and financing indicators, not facility-level continuity or clinical outcome measures (evidence: `who-unicef-wash-hcf-tracker-2025`).
- **Verified fact:** WHO's 2024 IPC report treats WASH as a condition supporting infection-prevention practice and reports an updated global analysis of IPC implementation. It does not identify the causal effect of a particular primary-care water or hand-hygiene intervention in a named LMIC (evidence: `who-ipc-global-report-2024`).

## Uncertain Areas

- Whether a facility's reported water source remains available during delivery, wound care, cleaning, vaccination, or other named care episodes outside the survey visit.
- Whether an improved source provides sufficient quantity and safe quality after pumping, storage, transport, treatment, or recontamination.
- Whether hand-hygiene supplies are available at all relevant points of care and toilets, and whether missing supplies reflect stock-outs, staff practice, or observation timing.
- Whether one-time self-report, direct observation, logbook, sensor, water-quality, and patient-exit measures agree over the same facility and time window.
- Whether HHFA, SARA, SPA, JMP, WASH FIT, and national facility surveys use compatible facility frames, service definitions, observation rules, and missingness handling.
- Whether facility-level WASH conditions predict patient or population outcomes after accounting for staffing, electricity, financing, case mix, referral, season, and facility selection; this pack does not assume that relationship.
- Whether a country already has a public or shareable facility-level dataset linking service continuity, maintenance, supplies, and point-of-care function strongly enough to make this discovery redundant.

## Initial Scope

This pack accepts:

- dated evidence records for health-care-facility water and hand-hygiene definitions, facility audits, continuity, quality, quantity, reliability, supplies, and repair;
- country-bounded or district-bounded source crosswalks that preserve facility type, managing authority, rurality, service area, sampling frame, observation date, season, and missingness;
- separate measurement families for source presence, on-premises location, availability when needed, quantity, quality, storage, pump or pipe function, hand-hygiene station function, supplies, and replenishment;
- primary-care analyses using de-identified, synthetic, or authorized facility records with explicit data ownership and consent boundaries;
- negative results showing that a proposed cross-source comparison or facility allocation cannot be audited; and
- red-team review of facility blame, privacy leakage, coercive targeting, patient exclusion, unsafe public disclosure, and false reassurance.

This pack does not accept:

- plumbing, procurement, financing, clinical, infection-prevention, or emergency-response instructions without the required domain, red-team, field-reality, and replication gates;
- a claim that a static water or hand-hygiene indicator proves continuous service, safe water, staff compliance, reduced infection, reduced mortality, or improved care quality;
- treating a facility audit as a population access or patient outcome estimate;
- facility, district, or country rankings used to punish staff, deny services, direct policing, or allocate funds before missingness and selection are reviewed;
- combining water, sanitation, electricity, waste, environmental cleaning, and clinical outcomes into a single score without an explicit measurement dictionary; or
- identifiable patient, worker, facility-security, water-source, or infrastructure-vulnerability data without authorization, minimization, access control, and an exit plan.

## V0 Done Condition

V0 is useful when one named country or health-system network has a reviewed and independently replicated facility artifact that distinguishes water and hand-hygiene presence, point-of-care function, availability when needed, quality or quantity where measured, stock-out or repair states, sampling frame, missingness, and the specific infrastructure or maintenance decision the evidence can inform without issuing a procurement or clinical order.
