# Health-Facility Electricity Reliability In Low- And Middle-Income Countries

## Problem Statement

Electricity access in a health facility is not a single state. A facility can be connected to a grid, have power during a survey visit, own a generator or solar system, and still be unable to keep a maternity ward, vaccine refrigerator, laboratory, water pump, communications system, or diagnostic device operating when the service is needed.

The missing operational evidence is not whether electricity matters. It is whether the available measurements can tell a named health-system decision-maker which failure mode is binding at a facility or service-area level:

> Which health facilities have electricity that is present, functional, reliable, backup-capable, and sufficient for a named service, and which of those measurements are strong enough to change a connection, maintenance, load-upgrade, or monitoring decision without being misread as proof of quality care?

## Decision Wedge

The primary decision users are a ministry of health infrastructure or biomedical-engineering unit, a rural electrification agency, and a health funder deciding among:

1. a new grid or off-grid connection;
2. repair, fuel, battery, or maintenance support for an existing source;
3. a backup system or load-capacity upgrade for a named service area; or
4. a better facility-monitoring and outage-data system before capital allocation.

The evidence that would change the decision is a reviewed and independently replicated facility table that keeps these states separate:

1. any electricity source present;
2. electricity functional in the observed service area at the visit;
3. outage frequency and duration over a stated window;
4. primary and backup source type, functionality, fuel or battery availability, and repair delay;
5. capacity to run the named service or device during interruption; and
6. the service actually observed, such as lighting, refrigeration, water pumping, sterilization, communications, or diagnostics.

The output must expose the sampling frame, facility level, urban/rural status, private/public coverage, missingness, measurement mode, and season. It must not convert an infrastructure gap map into a procurement order, a facility blame list, a clinical recommendation, or a mortality estimate.

## Strongest Objection And Response

**Objection:** WHO already publishes an electricity indicator, a global health-facility electrification database, and a financing report. A new pack could duplicate an established field instead of redirecting effort.

**Response:** The objection is valid about policy attention but not about decision-grade measurement. WHO's indicator is survey-based, reports only countries with available data, excludes facilities without reliability information from the denominator, and notes that surveys use different outage windows and sometimes ask about continuous power without defining a duration. The indicator is therefore a useful starting point, not a harmonized observation of whether a particular service area or device can operate through an interruption. A negative result is valuable here: if a named country already has comparable facility-level uptime, load, backup, maintenance, and service-use fields, the pack should document that the measurement gap is smaller than assumed and move to replication rather than invent another index.

## Known Facts

- **Verified fact:** WHO estimates that close to 1 billion people in low- and lower-middle-income countries are served by health-care facilities without reliable electricity or with no electricity access. In the WHO's available survey set, 12% of South Asian facilities and 15% of sub-Saharan African facilities have no electricity, and only 40% of sub-Saharan African facilities report reliable electricity; about half of hospitals in the region report reliable electricity ([WHO electricity fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/electricity-in-health-care-facilities)).
- **Verified fact:** WHO defines its `HCF_REL_ELECTRICITY` indicator as the percentage of facilities with a reliable electricity source, where reliable means no outage or interruption longer than two hours at a time during regular service hours in the prior week or two weeks. The database draws on national surveys and censuses, including SPA, SARA, SDI, HeRAMS, and EmONC, and excludes facilities without reliability information from the denominator ([WHO GHO indicator metadata](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/hcf-electricity--percentage-of-health-care-facilities-with-access-to-reliable-electricity-%28-%29)).
- **Verified fact:** WHO's HHFA resource package is designed for standardized facility surveys and includes service availability, service readiness, quality and safety, and management and finance modules. Its combined core questionnaire includes observed questions about whether electricity is functioning in a service area and whether backup power is available ([HHFA core questions](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/harmonized-health-facility-assessment-%28hhfa%29)).
- **Verified fact:** In a mixed-methods study of 44 public Malawian health-care facilities, most facilities were grid-connected but reported weekly interruptions averaging about 10 hours, and fewer than one-third had a functional backup source. The study associated inadequate energy availability with irregular water supply, poor sterilization, and unsafe working conditions; it was not a population-representative or causal outcome study ([Reuland et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz118)).
- **Verified fact:** A 2020 Ghana/Uganda implementation study used facility checklists, records, interviews, and community focus groups around a rural solar-electrification programme. It observed improvements in selected readiness measures, while statistically significant changes in recorded antenatal visits and deliveries were not observed; the study does not establish a general mortality effect ([Javadi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czaa077)).
- **Verified fact:** A 2026 secondary analysis of Malawi's 2019 HHFA included 596 facilities and reported stable-grid, unstable-grid, and non-grid categories alongside ultrasound availability and functionality. It found an infrastructure association, not a causal estimate of diagnostic access or health outcomes ([Ngongoma et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44401-025-00055-y)).

## Uncertain Areas

- Whether self-reported reliability over one or two weeks agrees with outage logs, smart-meter data, generator or battery records, and direct observation over the service's actual operating hours.
- Whether electricity is sufficient for a named load, rather than merely present somewhere on the facility compound or in one service area.
- How fuel shortages, battery degradation, repair delay, spare-parts access, load growth, seasonal hydropower conditions, and extreme weather alter reliability between survey rounds.
- Whether public facility surveys cover private, faith-based, mobile, conflict-affected, or newly opened facilities in the catchment denominator.
- Whether any observed relationship between power reliability and service readiness is confounded by staffing, roads, financing, facility level, demand, or programme selection.
- Whether a facility-level output would change a real infrastructure or maintenance decision without creating a blame or exclusion mechanism for facilities with missing data.

## Initial Scope

This pack accepts:

- Evidence inventories and source-identity checks for facility electricity data.
- Measurement dictionaries separating presence, function, reliability, backup, capacity, service-area reach, maintenance, and device function.
- Country or health-system analyses using de-identified or synthetic facility records with explicit sampling frames and missingness.
- Comparisons of survey, logbook, sensor, and administrative outage measures without treating them as interchangeable.
- Red-team review of procurement, financing, facility ranking, clinical, privacy, and blame risks.
- Field-reality review with ministries of health, energy agencies, biomedical engineers, facility managers, and funders.

This pack does not accept:

- Procurement, financing, generator, solar, battery, or grid-expansion instructions.
- Claims that electricity access alone improves mortality, quality of care, patient safety, or cost-effectiveness.
- Facility or country rankings before domain review, red-team review, field-reality review, and independent replication.
- Use of a connection, installed device, donor delivery, or written plan as proof of functional or reliable service.
- Identifiable patient, worker, facility-security, or precise infrastructure-vulnerability data.

## V0 Done Condition

The pack is useful when one named country or health-system network has a reviewed and independently replicated facility-level artifact that distinguishes electricity presence, current function, outage reliability, backup function, load or service-area coverage, and missingness, and states which named infrastructure or maintenance decision the evidence can inform without issuing a procurement or clinical order.
