# Epilepsy Treatment Access And Primary-Care Cascade Gaps In Low- And Middle-Income Countries

## Problem statement

Epilepsy is a common chronic neurological condition with a large treatment gap in the settings where most people with epilepsy live. The broad fact is not the missing knowledge. The missing decision-grade object is a verified cascade showing where people are lost between active epilepsy, recognition, diagnosis, antiseizure-medicine initiation, uninterrupted refills, seizure control, follow-up, and appropriate referral.

> Which combinations of population, facility, medicine, and programme data can support a defensible epilepsy-care cascade in a named low- or middle-income setting without treating modeled burden, outlet stock, clinic attendance, or self-reported seizure status as interchangeable measures?

The first users are national epilepsy or noncommunicable-disease programme managers, essential-medicines procurement authorities, district primary-care planners, and funders deciding whether the next marginal resource should repair medicine continuity, task-shared diagnosis and follow-up, referral, or community support. The pack is useful only if its evidence changes that decision and remains bounded to the observed setting.

## Why this is a discovery candidate

WHO reports around 50 million people living with epilepsy worldwide, nearly 80% in low- and middle-income countries, and about three quarters of people with epilepsy in low-income countries not receiving needed treatment ([who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024)). WHO also states that most people with epilepsy can be diagnosed and treated at primary-care level without sophisticated equipment and that appropriately treated people may become seizure-free ([who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024)).

The strongest counterargument is that epilepsy is not unrecognized: WHO, ILAE, and national programmes have produced reports, guidelines, pilots, and a global action plan. That is why this pack is not framed as an awareness campaign or a request for generic research. Its wedge is the missing, reviewable measurement layer that can distinguish:

- the denominator of active epilepsy from a modeled prevalence estimate;
- recognition, screening, diagnosis, and registration;
- medicine policy or procurement from facility stock, dispensing, possession, and refill continuity;
- reported seizure control from clinician-assessed outcome;
- primary-care follow-up from specialist referral and surgery;
- missingness or care outside the observed system from treatment failure.

The 2012 WHO/Health Action International analysis found poor medicine availability and affordability in facility surveys across 46 countries, but it is cross-sectional and old. The Addis Ababa study demonstrates a current local measurement pattern, while a randomized Indian trial demonstrates that a delivery pathway can be evaluated through observed medication and seizure outcomes. None of these sources alone supplies a generalizable country cascade ([cameron-antiepileptic-medicines-46-countries-2012](evidence.md#cameron-antiepileptic-medicines-46-countries-2012), [hailemariam-asm-addis-ababa-2023](evidence.md#hailemariam-asm-addis-ababa-2023), [singh-home-based-epilepsy-care-2022](evidence.md#singh-home-based-epilepsy-care-2022)).

## Known facts

- **Verified fact:** WHO reports around 50 million people with epilepsy globally and nearly 80% living in low- and middle-income countries. This is global burden framing, not an observed national denominator ([who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024)).
- **Verified fact:** WHO reports that about three quarters of people with epilepsy in low-income countries may not receive needed treatment. This statement must not be silently applied to all LMICs ([who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024)).
- **Verified fact:** WHO states that most people with epilepsy can be diagnosed and treated in primary care without sophisticated equipment, and that training primary-care providers has reduced treatment gaps in pilot programmes ([who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-fact-sheet-2024), [who-epilepsy-public-health-imperative-2019](evidence.md#who-epilepsy-public-health-imperative-2019)).
- **Verified fact:** WHO's 2024 neurological-medicine report identifies epilepsy as a tracer condition for medicine unavailability, unaffordability, and health-system access barriers and names national, subnational, district, programme, and procurement users ([who-neurological-medicines-access-2024](evidence.md#who-neurological-medicines-access-2024)).
- **Verified fact:** A 46-country WHO/HAI facility-survey analysis found generic antiepileptic-medicine availability below 50% in the public sector for all studied medicines except diazepam injection ([cameron-antiepileptic-medicines-46-countries-2012](evidence.md#cameron-antiepileptic-medicines-46-countries-2012)).
- **Verified fact:** A selected-outlet study in Addis Ababa found 52% overall availability of lowest-priced generic antiseizure medicines and reported unaffordability under its wage-based method ([hailemariam-asm-addis-ababa-2023](evidence.md#hailemariam-asm-addis-ababa-2023)).
- **Verified fact:** A cluster-randomized trial in one resource-limited Indian setting found better medication use and seizure outcomes in a home-based care arm than in usual clinic care, demonstrating a testable delivery signal without proving general scalability ([singh-home-based-epilepsy-care-2022](evidence.md#singh-home-based-epilepsy-care-2022)).

## Uncertain areas

- Whether a named setting has enough observed data to estimate a valid active-epilepsy denominator rather than a clinic-attending denominator.
- Whether treatment-gap estimates distinguish people never diagnosed from people diagnosed but unable to obtain, afford, or continue medicine.
- Whether medicine availability is persistent across time and facility tiers or is only a one-day outlet observation.
- Whether refill continuity, patient possession, adherence, and seizure control can be linked without exposing identifiable health data.
- Whether a cascade artifact changes a real procurement, workforce, or primary-care planning decision rather than merely producing a more attractive map.
- Whether people using private, traditional, faith-based, community, or informal care are missing from the observed system and therefore misclassified as untreated.

## Initial scope

1. Inventory sources that can measure burden, diagnosis, medicine access, primary-care contact, follow-up, referral, and outcomes.
2. Define a stage-level measure dictionary with explicit numerator, denominator, event date, grain, and missingness categories.
3. Build one setting-specific baseline only after the source inventory and measure dictionary pass review.
4. Red-team clinical, privacy, equity, and resource-allocation misuse before any programme-facing interpretation.
5. Check the artifact against one named decision-maker, decision date, and action boundary with a field-reality reviewer.

## Non-use boundaries

This pack does not publish patient-level diagnoses, medication or dose recommendations, seizure forecasts, individual driving or employment advice, automatic country rankings, or procurement orders. A treatment-gap estimate is not evidence that a particular person needs a particular medicine. Outlet stock is not delivered care. A clinic registry is not population prevalence. A modeled estimate is not a local case count.

## V0 done condition

One independently replicated cascade artifact for one named setting that reports stage-specific denominators, data provenance, missingness, and uncertainty from active-epilepsy ascertainment through diagnosis, medicine continuity, seizure outcome, follow-up, and referral, with domain, red-team, and field-reality review before publication.

## Discovery kill condition

Archive or substantially narrow this pack if a reviewed source inventory finds that existing national or global systems already provide a reproducible, setting-level cascade with compatible active-epilepsy denominators, diagnosis, medicine continuity, seizure outcomes, follow-up, and referral measures for the intended allocation decision. A second kill condition is evidence that the proposed cascade cannot be linked without identifiable health data or cannot change a named procurement or primary-care planning decision after field-reality review.
